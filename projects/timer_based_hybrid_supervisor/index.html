Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor for Policy Switching

Preventing Rapid Decision Chattering Under Measurement Noise via Predictive Dwell-Time Supervision

📄 Reinforcement Learning Conference (RLC 2025)

This article provides an accessible overview of our paper, "A Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor for Robust, Chatter-Free Policy Switching" by Jan de Priester and Ricardo G. Sanfelice, published in the Reinforcement Learning Journal (RLC 2025). For the complete mathematical definitions, hybrid Lyapunov analysis, and formal proofs, see the full paper and simulation code: [Read Paper PDF →] [Simulation Code →]

In many complex control and decision-making problems—ranging from autonomous obstacle avoidance and multi-target robotic navigation to tactical strategy selection—an agent must choose among a bank of specialized policies $\Pi = {\pi_1, \pi_2, \dots, \pi_N}$, each paired with a learned approximate value function $\hat{V}_q(z)$ for $q \in \mathcal{Q} := {1, \dots, N}$.

A natural way to orchestrate these policies is greedy value-maximizing supervision, which continuously selects the policy with the highest expected return at the current state:

\[Q^*(z) := \Big\{ q \in \mathcal{Q} : \hat{V}_q(z) = \max_{\bar{q} \in \mathcal{Q}} \hat{V}_{\bar{q}}(z) \Big\}\]

While greedy supervision preserves the stabilizing properties of the individual policies under ideal noise-free conditions, it suffers from a critical vulnerability in the real world: near boundaries where multiple policies appear equally optimal, small measurement noise or adversarial perturbations trick the supervisor into rapidly oscillating between conflicting decisions (chattering), resulting in stalls, limit-cycle traps, or collisions.

Existing chattering-mitigation strategies in reinforcement learning face two main limitations:

  • Spatial Partitioning (e.g., Overlapping Hysteresis Regions): Partitioning the state space into overlapping regions around switching boundaries works well when the geometry of the critical states is structured and known a priori (as in our 2D MultiHyRL and 3D L-MultiHyRL work), but becomes intractable when decision boundaries are fragmented or unknown in advance.
  • Fixed-Timer Switching: Enforcing a static minimum waiting time between policy switches creates a rigid dilemma: setting the timer too short fails to prevent chattering under measurement noise, while setting it too long yields overly conservative behavior that clings to suboptimal policies or traps the system bouncing between decision regions.

In this work, we resolve both limitations by introducing a predictive timer-based hybrid supervisor:

Rather than relying on prior spatial partitions or a static dwell timer, our supervisor predicts how long to hold the active policy. Using the learned value functions and a nominal forward model, the supervisor adaptively sets a resettable dwell timer $\delta_d$ at each switch—committing to the active policy until both the timer expires and a significantly better alternative emerges.

1. Why Value-Maximizing Supervisors Chatter Near Critical Areas

Consider a continuous-time system $\dot{z} = f(z, u)$ controlled by selecting a policy $\pi_q \in \Pi$ from a bank of Lipschitz continuous policies, each globally asymptotically stabilizing a target set $\mathcal{Z}_q^*$ with a continuously differentiable neural value approximation $\hat{V}_q(z)$.

Why does selecting $q \in Q^(z)$ chatter under small measurement noise $m(t)$? Chattering originates around critical areas $\mathcal{M}^ \subset \mathcal{Z}$, which we formally characterize by three value-function conditions between competing policies $q, p \in Q^*(z)$ ($q \neq p$):

  1. Equal Optimal Value: Multiple policies yield the exact same maximal return at state $z$: \(\hat{V}_q(z) = \hat{V}_p(z)\)
  2. Local Improvement Under the Chosen Policy: Following either policy locally improves its own value function along the closed-loop trajectory: \(\langle \nabla \hat{V}_q(z), f(z, \pi_q(z)) \rangle > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \langle \nabla \hat{V}_p(z), f(z, \pi_p(z)) \rangle > 0\)
  3. Local Degradation Under the Opposing Policy: Applying the competing policy locally decreases the other policy’s value function: \(\langle \nabla \hat{V}_q(z), f(z, \pi_p(z)) \rangle < 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \langle \nabla \hat{V}_p(z), f(z, \pi_q(z)) \rangle < 0\)

Together, these three conditions mean that near $\mathcal{M}^$, the state space is split into adjacent regions $\mathcal{Z}_q$ and $\mathcal{Z}_p$ where the two policies pull the system in opposing directions. When the state $z$ lies within $\delta$ of the boundary $\mathcal{M}^$, a small sensor perturbation $m$ of magnitude $\varepsilon \ge \delta$ shifts the measured state $z + m$ across the boundary into $\mathcal{Z}_p$. Selecting $\pi_p$ then drives the true state $z$ back toward the boundary $\mathcal{M}^$, making the critical area *attractive under alternating noise and trapping the system in high-frequency chattering.

Motivating Example: Stabilizing Two Disconnected Setpoints on a Line

To see this mechanism clearly, consider a 1D system $\dot{z} = u \in [-1, 1]$ tasked with stabilizing two disconnected setpoints $\mathcal{Z}^* = {z_1^, z_2^} = {-1, +1}$ using two policies $\pi_1(z) = z_1^* - z$ and $\pi_2(z) = z_2^* - z$.

Left: Approximate value functions $\hat{V}_1$ (blue) and $\hat{V}_2$ (green) for policies $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$, with setpoints $\mathcal{Z}^* = \{-1, +1\}$ marked by red stars. Right: Resulting piecewise-continuous control input $u$ under the greedy supervisor $Q^*(z)$.

Inspecting the value functions and resulting control law above reveals two distinct types of switching boundaries:

  • Exploitative Switching at $z \approx \pm 1.6$ (Benign): Far from the origin ($z < -1.6$ or $z > 1.6$), $Q^(z)$ temporarily selects the *opposing policy ($\pi_2$ for $z < -1.6$, and $\pi_1$ for $z > 1.6$) because it produces a larger control input in the same direction toward $\mathcal{Z}^*$, accelerating convergence. Because both policies have the same sign here, chattering does not stall the system.
  • Critical State at $z_c = 0$ (Catastrophic Chattering): At $z_c = 0$, $\hat{V}_1(0) = \hat{V}_2(0)$, while $\pi_1(0) = -1 < 0$ and $\pi_2(0) = +1 > 0$ oppose each other. Under bounded measurement noise $m(t)$ of magnitude $\varepsilon = 0.3$ (middle panel below), $Q^(z + m)$ chatters rapidly between $q=1$ and $q=2$, trapping trajectories at $z = 0$ and preventing convergence to $\mathcal{Z}^$.
Left ($\varepsilon = 0$, Greedy $Q^*$): Without noise, all trajectories converge to $\mathcal{Z}^* = \{-1, +1\}$. Middle ($\varepsilon = 0.3$, Greedy $Q^*$): Bounded measurement noise causes trajectories starting near $z_c = 0$ to chatter around the origin and fail to stabilize. Right ($\varepsilon = 0.3$, Proposed Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor): With predictive dwell-time supervision ($\mu = 0.1, \bar{\delta}_d = 0.5$), chattering is eliminated both near $z_c = 0$ and near $z = \pm 1.5$, restoring smooth convergence.

2. How the Predictive Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor Works

To prevent chattering without requiring prior spatial partitions of $\mathcal{M}^*$, we augment the system state with three supervisory variables to form the hybrid state $x := (z, \tau, q, \delta_d) \in \mathcal{X}$:

  1. Timer $\tau \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$: Counts the elapsed time since the most recent policy switch ($\dot{\tau} = 1$).
  2. Active Policy Index $q \in \mathcal{Q}$: Locks in the currently deployed policy $\pi_q$ during continuous flow ($\dot{q} = 0$).
  3. Adaptive Dwell Time $\delta_d \in \mathbb{R}_{> 0}$: Specifies the minimum time that must elapse before the supervisor is allowed to switch policies ($\dot{\delta}_d = 0$).

Visualizing the Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor in a Perturbed 2-Obstacle Arena: Phase 1 (Nominal Forecast at $\tau = 0, q = 2$): Using a noise-free model, the supervisor propagates a nominal state forecast $\chi(\eta)$ (green dashed) under active policy $q = 2$ until alternative policy $q = 1$ becomes significantly better ($V_1(\chi(5)) > V_2(\chi(5))$), locking in a predicted dwell time of $\delta_d = 5$. Phase 2 (Perturbed Flow while $\tau < \delta_d = 5$): An environmental shift perturbs the first obstacle. A memoryless agent without a timer (red) chatters across the critical boundary and crashes, whereas our supervisor holds $q = 2$ until $\tau = 5$, safely clearing the obstacle to reach actual state $z(5)$. Phase 3 (Timer Expiration $\tau = 5 \to$ Switch & Re-Forecast): Once $\tau = \delta_d = 5$ and $V_1(z(5)) > V_2(z(5))$, the supervisor jumps to $q^+ = 1$, resets $\tau^+ = 0$, and predicts a new dwell time $\delta_d^+ = 10$ from $z(5)$ to safely bypass the second obstacle.

The Closed-Loop Hybrid System $\mathcal{H} = (C, F, D, G)$

The interaction between continuous control execution and discrete policy switching is governed by the hybrid system $\mathcal{H}$:

  • Continuous Flow ($x \in C := C_0 \cup C_1$): While the state evolves continuously under the active policy $\dot{z} = f(z, \pi_q(z))$ and the timer increments at unit rate $\dot{\tau} = 1$, the supervisor keeps $q$ and $\delta_d$ constant as long as either the dwell timer has not yet expired or the active policy is still optimal: \(C_0 := \{x \in \mathcal{X} : \tau \le \delta_d\}, \qquad C_1 := \{x \in \mathcal{X} : q \in Q^*(z)\}\)
  • Discrete Policy Switch ($x \in D$): A policy switch is triggered only when both the dwell timer has expired and the active policy is no longer optimal: \(D := \big\{x \in \mathcal{X} : \tau \ge \delta_d,\; q \in \mathcal{Q} \setminus Q^*(z)\big\}\)
  • Predictive Dwell-Time Update ($x^+ \in G(x)$): At a jump, the physical state remains unchanged ($z^+ = z$), the timer resets ($\tau^+ = 0$), the supervisor selects a newly optimal policy $q^+ = q’ \in Q^*(z)$, and the new dwell time $\delta_d^+$ is computed via forward prediction: \(\delta_d^+ = \min\Big(\mathcal{T}(z, q') \cup \{\bar{\delta}_d\}\Big)\) where $\mathcal{T}(z, q’)$ collects all future horizons $\eta \in [0, \bar{\delta}d]$ along the nominal noise-free trajectory $\dot{\chi} = f(\chi, \pi{q’}(\chi))$ (initialized at $\chi(0) = z$) at which a competing policy $\bar{q} \neq q’$ achieves a significant relative value improvement of at least $\mu > 0$: \(\mathcal{T}(z, q') := \left\{ \eta \in [0, \bar{\delta}_d] : \frac{\max_{\bar{q} \in \mathcal{Q} \setminus \{q'\}} \hat{V}_{\bar{q}}\big(\chi(\eta)\big) - \hat{V}_{q'}\big(\chi(\eta)\big)}{\big\lvert \hat{V}_{q'}\big(\chi(\eta)\big) \big\rvert + \epsilon} \ge \mu \right\}\)

Crucially, normalizing by $\lvert \hat{V}_{q’}(\chi(\eta)) \rvert + \epsilon$ (with $0 < \epsilon \ll 1$ preventing division by zero) ensures that $\mu > 0$ acts as a consistent fractional improvement threshold whether the value functions are positive or negative. If no alternative policy beats $q’$ by a relative margin of $\mu$ within the horizon $\bar{\delta}_d$ (i.e., $\mathcal{T}(z, q’) = \varnothing$), the dwell time defaults to the upper bound $\bar{\delta}_d$.

🔍 Formal Guarantees & Principled Parameter Selection

  • Well-Posed & Non-Zeno Execution (Propositions 1 & 2): Under continuous $\hat{V}_q$ and Lipschitz continuous $f$ and $\pi_q$, the hybrid closed-loop system $\mathcal{H}$ satisfies the Hybrid Basic Conditions. Every maximal solution is complete, non-Zeno, and exhibits jump times separated by a uniform minimum dwell period $t_{j+1} - t_j \ge \beta > 0$.
  • Stability Preservation & Chattering Robustness (Theorem 1): When each $\pi_q$ globally asymptotically stabilizes $\mathcal{Z}_q^*$, $\mathcal{H}$ renders the composite target set $\mathcal{Z}^* := \bigcup_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \mathcal{Z}_q^*$ robustly globally asymptotically stable and guarantees a nonzero robustness margin $\varepsilon > 0$ against measurement noise.
  • Principled Tuning of $\bar{\delta}_d$ and $\mu$ (Section 3.3): To guarantee that the state escapes the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of critical areas before reconsidering a switch, choose the maximum prediction horizon $\bar{\delta}_d$ and relative threshold $\mu$ to exceed the worst-case noise perturbations: $$\bar{\delta}_d > \frac{\varepsilon}{\max_{(z,u) \in X} \lvert f(z,u) \rvert}, \qquad \mu > \frac{\varepsilon L_{\hat{V}}}{\lvert \hat{V}_{\text{ref}} \rvert}$$ where $L_{\hat{V}} := \max_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} L_{\hat{V}_q}$ is the Lipschitz constant of the value networks and $\lvert \hat{V}_{\text{ref}} \rvert$ is a characteristic value scale on the operational domain $X$.

3. Multi-Target Navigation in a Rugged 2D Reward Landscape

To test our supervisor in a setting where spatial state-space partitioning is difficult to apply, we consider a 2D planar system $\dot{z} = u \in [-1, 1]^2$ with state $z = (z_x, z_y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ tasked with stabilizing four disconnected setpoints $\mathcal{Z}^* = {z_1^, z_2^, z_3^, z_4^}$ using four policies $\pi_1, \dots, \pi_4$. We train the approximate value functions $\hat{V}_1, \dots, \hat{V}_4$ on a reward landscape featuring periodic spatial variations:

\[R(z) = -c_1 \lvert z \rvert_{\mathcal{Z}^*} \left(1 + \frac{1}{2}\sin(c_2 z_x)\cos(c_3 z_y)\right)\]

These periodic variations create local minima and maxima across the plane, meaning that following the shortest Euclidean path to a setpoint is no longer optimal. As shown below, this transforms the greedy policy regions $Q^*(z)$ from clean geometric partitions (left) into highly fragmented, non-convex decision islands (right):

Left (Without Periodic Variations): The value-maximizing regions $Q^*(z)$ form clean, predictable partitions around the four setpoints (red stars). Right (With Periodic Variations): Local reward peaks and valleys fracture $Q^*(z)$ into intricate, non-convex switching boundaries where pre-computing overlapping spatial partitions is nontrivial.

We evaluate closed-loop trajectories on this rugged landscape under adversarial measurement noise $m(t) = [\varepsilon, -\varepsilon]^\top m_{\text{sgn}}(t)$ of magnitude $\varepsilon = 0.1$ across three supervisory strategies:

Closed-loop trajectories (black dashed lines; circles mark initial states, crosses mark terminal states) and distance to target $\lvert z^* - z \rvert$ over time under measurement noise ($\varepsilon = 0.1$). Left (Greedy $Q^*$): Severe chattering across fragmented boundaries prevents multiple trajectories from reaching $\mathcal{Z}^*$. Middle (Fixed-Timer Supervisor, $\delta_d \equiv 0.5$): Prevents rapid chattering, but trajectories near $(-0.25, \pm 0.25)$ become trapped bouncing indefinitely between decision regions. Right (Proposed Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor, $\bar{\delta}_d = 0.5, \mu = 0.15$): Adaptively predicts dwell times to eliminate chattering and smoothly guides all initial conditions to $\mathcal{Z}^*$.
  • Failure of Greedy Supervision (Left Plot): Without temporal protection, measurement noise along the fragmented boundaries triggers rapid switching among $q \in {1, 2, 3, 4}$, stalling several trajectories far from the target setpoints.
  • Why a Fixed Timer is Not Enough (Middle Plot): Imposing a constant dwell time of $\delta_d \equiv 0.5$ eliminates high-frequency chattering, but because the timer ignores where the trajectory is heading, solutions starting near $(-0.25, 0.25)$ and $(-0.25, -0.25)$ repeatedly overshoot into neighboring regions and switch back—getting trapped in a persistent oscillation and failing to converge.
  • Success of Predictive Dwell-Time Supervision (Right Plot): By forecasting when a competing policy will offer at least a $\mu = 15\%$ relative value gain, our timer-based hybrid supervisor switches at the right moments and drives every evaluated initial condition smoothly to the target set $\mathcal{Z}^*$.

🛠️ Practical Engineering Takeaway: When to Use Timer Supervision vs. Spatial Hysteresis

Across our hybrid RL framework, spatial hysteresis (such as 2D MultiHyRL and 3D L-MultiHyRL) and predictive timer supervision offer complementary tools for chatter-free autonomy. When a single dynamics model is unknown or expensive to roll out online—such as vision-driven 3D flight—spatial hysteresis over learned CNN features provides zero-rollout switching. Conversely, when an agent switches among arbitrary learned policies with complex, fragmented value boundaries where critical regions cannot be partitioned in advance, our Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor guarantees chatter-free stability using only value evaluations along short forward predictions.

For the complete mathematical proofs on outer semicontinuity of the predictive jump map, non-Zeno hybrid time domains, and the hybrid Lyapunov stability analysis, please refer to our RLC 2025 paper (de Priester & Sanfelice, 2025).
