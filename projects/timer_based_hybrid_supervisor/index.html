Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor for Policy Switching

Preventing Rapid Decision Chattering Under Measurement Noise via Predictive Dwell-Time Supervision

📄 Reinforcement Learning Conference (RLC 2025)

This article provides an accessible overview of our paper, "A Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor for Robust, Chatter-Free Policy Switching" by Jan de Priester and Ricardo G. Sanfelice, published in the Reinforcement Learning Journal (RLC 2025). For the complete mathematical definitions, hybrid Lyapunov analysis, and formal proofs, see the full paper and simulation code: [Read Paper PDF →] [Simulation Code →]

In many complex control and decision-making problems—ranging from autonomous obstacle avoidance and multi-target robotic navigation to multi-skill locomotion and manipulation—a single control policy is not enough. Instead, an autonomous agent often relies on a bank of specialized reinforcement learning policies, each trained with its own value function to handle a specific maneuver, target, or operating mode.

A natural way to coordinate multiple policies at runtime is greedy value-maximizing supervision: at every instant, the supervisor evaluates the learned value functions at the current state and activates whichever policy promises the highest expected return.

While greedy switching works well under ideal noise-free conditions, it suffers from a critical failure mode when deployed in the real world: near decision boundaries where two or more policies appear equally optimal, small sensor noise or environmental perturbations trick the supervisor into rapidly oscillating between conflicting policies (chattering), resulting in stalls, limit-cycle traps, or collisions.

Existing strategies to prevent policy chattering face a practical dilemma:

  • Spatial Hysteresis Partitioning: Constructing overlapping spatial regions around switching boundaries is highly effective when critical regions have a structured geometry (as in our Hysteresis-Based Reinforcement Learning work for obstacle avoidance), but becomes intractable when value-function boundaries are complex, fragmented, or unknown in advance.
  • Fixed-Timer Switching: Enforcing a constant minimum waiting time between policy switches creates a rigid trade-off: setting the timer too short fails to stop chattering under sensor noise, while setting it too long makes the agent unresponsive and traps it bouncing between decision regions.

In this work, we resolve both limitations by introducing a predictive timer-based hybrid supervisor:

Rather than relying on pre-computed spatial partitions or a fixed waiting time, our supervisor uses the learned value functions to predict how long to hold the active policy. At each switch, the supervisor forecasts when a competing policy will become significantly better along a nominal forward rollout—locking in an adaptive dwell timer $\delta_d$ that eliminates chattering while preserving agile, globally stabilizing performance.

1. Why Greedy Supervisors Chatter—and When Switching is Actually Beneficial

Why does selecting the policy with the highest value function $Q^\ast(z)$ cause catastrophic chattering in some regions of the state space, while working harmlessly in others?

In our paper, we show that switching boundaries between competing policies fall into two distinct categories:

  1. Exploitative Switching (Benign): Far from a target, two policies may temporarily trade leadership even though both push the system in the same general direction (for example, when one policy saturates the actuators more aggressively toward the goal). Switching here actually accelerates convergence, and noise near this boundary does not stall the agent.
  2. Critical Areas $\mathcal{M}^\ast$ (Catastrophic Chattering): Along critical areas, two policies yield the exact same optimal value ($\hat{V}\sb{1}(z)$ equals $\hat{V}\sb{2}(z)$), but their control actions pull the system in opposing directions. When the true state $z$ approaches $\mathcal{M}^\ast$, small measurement noise of magnitude $\varepsilon$ pushes the perceived state across the boundary, activating the opposing policy and driving the state right back toward the boundary. Under alternating sensor noise, the critical boundary becomes an artificial trap that locks the system into high-frequency chattering.

2. How Predictive Dwell-Time Supervision Works

To prevent chattering without needing prior geometric maps of the critical areas $\mathcal{M}^\ast$, our supervisor augments the control loop with a resettable timer $\tau$, the active policy index $q$, and an adaptive dwell time $\delta_d$:

Animation: Predictive Dwell-Time Supervision vs. Memoryless Chattering ● Three-Phase Hybrid Execution
Visualizing the Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor in a Perturbed 2-Obstacle Arena: Phase 1 (Nominal Forecast): Using a nominal forward model, the supervisor rolls out a predicted trajectory (green dashed) under active policy $q = 2$ until alternative policy $q = 1$ offers a significantly higher value, locking in a predicted dwell time of $\delta_d = 5$. Phase 2 (Perturbed Execution): An environmental disturbance shifts the first obstacle. A memoryless agent without a timer (red) chatters across the critical boundary and crashes, whereas our supervisor holds $q = 2$ until the timer reaches $\tau = 5$, safely clearing the obstacle. Phase 3 (Timer Expiration & Re-Forecast): Once $\tau = \delta_d = 5$ and policy $q = 1$ has higher value at the true state, the supervisor switches to $q^+ = 1$, resets $\tau^+ = 0$, and predicts a new dwell time $\delta_d^+ = 10$ to bypass the second obstacle.

At runtime, the closed-loop hybrid system operates through a clean, two-part mechanism:

  • Continuous Execution (Commitment While Timer Runs): The agent applies the locked-in policy $\pi_q$ and increments the timer $\tau$ continuously as long as either the adaptive dwell time has not yet elapsed ($\tau \le \delta_d$) or the active policy remains optimal under the value functions. This guarantees that measurement noise cannot trigger premature switching while the agent is crossing a vulnerable critical boundary.
  • Predictive Dwell-Time Update at Each Switch: When the dwell timer has expired ($\tau \ge \delta_d$) and a better policy $q’$ emerges, the supervisor switches to $q’$, resets the timer ($\tau^+ = 0$), and simulates a short nominal trajectory forward in time (up to a maximum horizon $\bar{\delta}\sb{d}$). The new dwell time $\delta_d^+$ is set to the first predicted time at which another policy beats $q’$ by a relative value margin of at least $\mu > 0$ (or defaults to $\bar{\delta}\sb{d}$ if no policy beats $q’$ within the horizon).

🔍 Key Theoretical Guarantees (In Plain Language)

  • Guaranteed Chattering Elimination (Non-Zeno Execution): Because every switch enforces a strictly positive minimum dwell period, the closed-loop hybrid system is provably free of high-frequency chattering and Zeno executions.
  • Robust Global Asymptotic Stability (Theorem 1): When each individual policy stabilizes its target set, the timer-based hybrid supervisor preserves global asymptotic stability of the combined target set and provides a formal robustness margin $\varepsilon > 0$ against measurement noise.
  • Principled Tuning of the Horizon $\bar{\delta}\sb{d}$ and Threshold $\mu$: The paper provides explicit, intuitive bounds showing how to choose the maximum horizon $\bar{\delta}\sb{d}$ (large enough for the system to cross the noise band around $\mathcal{M}^\ast$) and relative margin $\mu$ (large enough to reject value-function fluctuations caused by noise $\varepsilon$).

3. Application: Multi-Target Navigation in a Rugged Reward Landscape

To demonstrate the impact of predictive dwell-time supervision in environments where spatial partitioning is impractical, we evaluate a 2D autonomous agent navigating toward four target setpoints across a rugged, spatially varying reward landscape:

\[R(z) = -c_1 \lvert z \rvert_{\mathcal{Z}^\ast} \Big(1 + 0.5\sin(c_2 z_x)\cos(c_3 z_y)\Big)\]

Because the periodic peaks and valleys in the reward landscape make straight-line paths suboptimal, the greedy policy regions $Q^\ast(z)$ fracture from clean geometric quadrants (left figure below) into highly fragmented, non-convex decision islands (right figure below):

Left (Uniform Distance Reward): The greedy policy regions $Q^\ast(z)$ form four clean quadrants around the target setpoints (red stars). Right (Rugged Periodic Reward Landscape): Local reward peaks and valleys fracture $Q^\ast(z)$ into intricate, non-convex switching boundaries where pre-computing spatial hysteresis partitions is intractable.

When deployed on this rugged landscape under adversarial measurement noise ($\varepsilon = 0.1$), the three supervisory strategies exhibit dramatically different behavior:

Closed-loop trajectories (black dashed lines; circles mark initial states, crosses mark terminal states) and target distance over time under measurement noise ($\varepsilon = 0.1$). Left (Greedy $Q^\ast$): Severe chattering across fragmented boundaries stalls multiple trajectories far from the targets. Middle (Fixed-Timer Supervisor, $\delta_d = 0.5$): Stops rapid chattering, but trajectories starting near $(-0.25, \pm 0.25)$ become trapped bouncing indefinitely between decision regions. Right (Proposed Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor): Adaptively predicts dwell times to eliminate chattering and smoothly guides every initial condition to the target set.
  • Greedy Supervision Stalls (Left Plot): Without temporal memory, sensor noise along the fragmented boundaries triggers rapid chattering among the four policies, trapping several trajectories far from the targets.
  • Fixed-Timer Supervision Gets Trapped (Middle Plot): Holding every policy for a fixed duration ($\delta_d = 0.5$) prevents high-frequency chattering, but because a fixed timer ignores where the trajectory is heading, agents starting near $(-0.25, \pm 0.25)$ repeatedly overshoot into neighboring regions and switch back—getting trapped in a persistent oscillation.
  • Predictive Timer Supervision Succeeds (Right Plot): By forecasting when a competing policy will offer at least a $\mu = 15\%$ relative value gain, our supervisor switches at the right moments and drives every evaluated initial condition smoothly to the target set.

🛠️ Practical Impact & Applications: Choosing Between Spatial Hysteresis and Timer Supervision

Together, our Timer-Based Hybrid Supervisor and our Hysteresis-Based Reinforcement Learning (HyRL / MultiHyRL / L-MultiHyRL) framework provide two complementary tools for safe, chatter-free autonomy:

  • Use Spatial Hysteresis (MultiHyRL / L-MultiHyRL) when operating model-free from high-dimensional onboard perception (such as 3D vision-based drone obstacle avoidance), where overlapping regions around obstacles can be classified directly at runtime without forward dynamics rollouts.
  • Use Predictive Timer Supervision (TimerHyRL) in hierarchical multi-policy control, multi-target routing, and tactical decision-making, where an agent switches among arbitrary learned policies with complex, fragmented value boundaries that cannot be partitioned spatially in advance.

For the complete mathematical formulation—including outer semicontinuity of the predictive jump map, non-Zeno hybrid time domains, and the hybrid Lyapunov stability proofs—please refer to our RLC 2025 paper (de Priester & Sanfelice, 2025).
